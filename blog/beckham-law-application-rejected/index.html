Beckham Law application rejected: the 6 most common reasons (and how to avoid them)

JL

Javier López Founder · holds the Beckham regime · not a lawyer

Published: 25 August 2026 · Updated: 6 September 2026 · 7 min read
requirementsForm 149tax residence

Meeting the Beckham Law requirements is not enough, you have to be able to prove them, and avoid procedural own-goals. The requirements are cumulative: failing any single one invalidates the election, however strong the rest of the case is. These are the rejection causes we see most, in order of frequency, followed by what a rejection actually looks like and how to keep your file out of that pile.

1. Filing after the deadline

The classic. Form 149 must be filed within 6 months of your Social Security registration (or of the certificate of coverage that lets you keep your home-country system), and there are no extensions. The deadline is preclusive: a late filing is rejected without any assessment of the merits. We cover the mechanics, the trigger date for each route and the narrow options if you are late in our 6-month deadline guide.

How to avoid it: write down the trigger date the day it happens and file in the first weeks, not the last.

2. Spanish tax residence in the previous 5 years

The requirement: you must not have been a Spanish tax resident in the 5 tax years before the year you relocate (the Startup Law cut the look-back from 10 years to 5). The usual stumbles:

  • Past stays that were residence without you realising: a one-year master’s, a long exchange, six months of remote work from the coast… If you crossed 183 days in a calendar year, you were probably a tax resident, whatever your visa said.
  • Being unable to prove residence elsewhere: the tax office can demand tax-residence certificates from the countries where you lived. Getting them retroactively from some jurisdictions (UAE, Singapore, certain US setups) can be slow or impossible without planning.
  • Family in Spain: a spouse and minor children living in Spain can trigger a presumption that you were resident, even if you spent few days here.
  • Economic centre of interests: property, a business or the bulk of your income in Spain during a previous year can make you resident even under 183 days.

What AEAT looks at: municipal registrations, previous Spanish returns or withholdings, property records, bank and card activity, and your family’s whereabouts. How to avoid it: reconstruct the last five years on paper before filing, and request residence certificates early.

3. Registering as self-employed too early

The regime requires your move to be caused by a listed circumstance: an employment contract, an employer-ordered relocation, a directorship, international remote work, or a certified entrepreneurial or highly-qualified activity. Registering as an ordinary autónomo on arrival, to invoice “while looking for a job”, can break that causal link and close the door, because ordinary self-employment is not an access route and creates income through a permanent establishment.

How to avoid it: secure the access route first, everything else after. If you need to work for yourself, read the three routes that do work for the self-employed before registering anything.

4. The employer link doesn’t hold up

The relocation must be caused by the work, and the file must show it. Typical failures:

  • Contracts signed after the move with no evidence the relocation was work-caused: you arrived, looked around, found a job. The chronology has to support the story.
  • Intra-group transfers without an assignment letter: the foreign employer keeps paying you and nobody documented the posting to Spain.
  • Remote workers without the digital-nomad permit: the permit creates a legal presumption that the remote-work requirement is met. Without it (EU citizens, for instance), the burden of proving a genuine, exclusively remote employment relationship with a foreign employer is entirely on you.
  • Your own company as the employer: a salary from your own foreign LLC or S-corp can fit the remote-work route, but AEAT scrutinises substance and salary structure far more closely. See Beckham Law with a US LLC or S-corp.

How to avoid it: date the contract or assignment letter before the move, keep the posting documented, and get the permit where it applies.

5. Incomplete or inconsistent paperwork

Form 149 travels with supporting documents: contract or assignment letter, Social Security registration or certificate of coverage, passport, NIE. AEAT reads them side by side. Dates that don’t line up across contract, registration and arrival; inconsistent names or addresses; a contract that says one employer and a payslip that says another; missing sworn translations, every inconsistency is an information request, and every request is time and risk against a 6-month clock.

How to avoid it: build the file as one coherent story and have somebody else read it cold before submitting.

6. A permanent establishment in Spain

If the tax office reads your income as coming from your own business activity carried out through a permanent establishment in Spain, the regime doesn’t apply (the certified entrepreneurial and highly-qualified routes are the exceptions). A real risk for profiles mixing employment with side projects, and for owner-managers whose “salary” looks like business profit.

How to avoid it: keep employment income and business income structurally separate, and get the structure reviewed before you move rather than after.

What a rejection actually looks like

Two things can happen after you file, and they are not the same.

An information request (requerimiento). AEAT asks for documents or clarifications. You typically have ten working days to answer. This is normal and not a rejection, but it is where most files are won or lost, because a weak or late answer becomes the ground for denial.

A denial (acuerdo de denegación). A reasoned decision stating which requirement, in AEAT’s view, you fail. It can be appealed: a reconsideration request to the same office within one month, or a claim to the regional economic-administrative tribunal within one month, and ultimately the courts. Appeals succeed when there is a real legal argument, a disputed trigger date, a misread residence history, a permit AEAT ignored, not as a way to fix a file that was simply incomplete.

The notification trap. Once you deal electronically with AEAT, notifications are delivered to your electronic mailbox and count as received ten calendar days after being made available, whether or not you open them. A request you never saw becomes a denial you cannot answer. Set up the electronic notification system, subscribe to email alerts, and check the mailbox weekly until the certificate arrives.

Rejection versus losing the regime later

A rejection means the election was never accepted. Losing the regime happens later, while you are inside it, when you stop meeting a requirement: you leave your employer and start invoicing clients, your company becomes an asset-holding entity above the 25% stake limit, you miss an annual Form 151. That breach must be notified to AEAT within one month, the regime ends, and it cannot be elected again. Voluntary renunciation works differently: it is filed in November–December of the year before it takes effect and is irrevocable.

Both roads end at the general income tax. The difference is that a rejection is usually a filing problem, and a later loss is usually a planning problem.

How to bulletproof your application

  • Reconstruct your residence history for the last 5 years before filing anything, with supporting documents.
  • Request tax-residence certificates from your previous countries early; some take months.
  • Check that contract, registration and moving dates tell one coherent story.
  • Set up and watch your electronic notifications from the day you file.
  • If your case has any edge (freelancing, a directorship with equity, remote work for your own company, a family split across countries, US citizenship), get a professional review before filing Form 149, not after the rejection.

Frequently asked questions

Can I reapply for the Beckham Law after a rejection? +

It depends on the reason. If it was fixable paperwork and you are still within the 6-month window, yes. If you failed a substantive requirement (prior residence, late filing), the rejection is hard to reverse, though appeals are possible where there are real arguments.

Does living in Spain as a student count as prior residence? +

What matters is tax residence, not visa type. A student who spent more than 183 days a year in Spain was most likely a tax resident, and that counts within the 5-year look-back period.

Does the tax office really check the 5 years of non-residence? +

Yes. It cross-references municipal registrations, past filings, property, bank accounts and card activity. If there are signs of prior residence, it will demand tax-residence certificates from the countries where you lived.

How long do I have to answer an information request from AEAT? +

Typically ten working days from notification. Electronic notifications count as delivered ten calendar days after they are made available even if you never open them, so make sure your electronic notifications are set up and monitored from the moment you file.

Is a rejection the same as losing the regime later? +

No. A rejection means the election was never accepted. Losing the regime later happens when you stop meeting a requirement while already inside it, for example by starting to earn income through a permanent establishment in Spain, and must be notified within one month. Both end at the general regime, but the causes and the remedies differ.
