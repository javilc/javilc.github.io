Self-employed and the Beckham Law: why freelancers don't qualify (and the 3 exceptions that do)

JL

Javier López Founder · holds the Beckham regime · not a lawyer

Published: 21 July 2026 · Updated: 6 September 2026 · 7 min read
self-employedStartup LawENISA

“I’m a freelancer, can I use the Beckham Law?” It’s probably the question we get most, and the short answer is: as an ordinary autónomo, no; but since the Startup Law (Law 28/2022) there are three routes that may fit you. Which one, and in what order you take the steps, decides whether you pay 24% or the progressive scale for the next six years.

Why the ordinary freelancer is excluded

Article 93 of the Spanish income tax law requires your move to Spain to be caused by a listed circumstance: an employment contract, an employer-ordered relocation, a directorship, remote employment for a foreign employer, or certain qualified self-employed activities. The regime also excludes anyone earning income through a permanent establishment in Spain, which is exactly what an ordinary freelancer invoicing clients from here creates.

There is a second, quieter reason. The 24% rate applies to employment income. Income from an economic activity is a different category in Spanish tax law, and the regime’s flat rate was never designed for it; the Startup Law carved out specific exceptions precisely because the general rule keeps the self-employed out.

Practical translation: if you land, register as an autónomo and start invoicing as a generalist consultant, there is no Beckham regime for you. Worse: that registration can contaminate a later application through another route, because it documents that your move was to run a business here, not to take up one of the listed circumstances.

Exception 1: entrepreneurial activity (with an ENISA report)

If your project is innovative and of special economic interest for Spain, you can enter the regime as an entrepreneur. The key is a favourable report from ENISA, the public company that assesses the innovative nature of the business, its scalability and its plan. It’s the natural route for founders building their startup from Spain.

What you need to show: a business plan that ENISA reads as innovative and scalable, a real activity (not a holding vehicle), and the report itself before AEAT asks for it.

Watch-outs: request the report before betting your move on this route; ENISA’s timelines are not instant; and the 6-month Form 149 clock keeps running from your registration regardless of how long ENISA takes. Plan the sequence so the report exists when you file, or file with the request pending and be ready to evidence it.

Exception 2: highly-qualified professional

Self-employed professionals qualify if they provide services to certified startups (empresas emergentes under the Startup Law) or carry out training, research, development and innovation activities, earning more than 40% of their total income from them. This is the route for technical and scientific profiles freelancing for the startup ecosystem: engineers, data scientists, researchers, product specialists.

What you need to show: that the client is a certified startup (the certification is public), or that the work is genuinely R&D&I; and that those services exceed 40% of your income, not only the year you apply, but every year you stay in the regime.

Watch-outs: the 40% threshold is measured over time, so a change in your client mix can push you out. Keep contracts and invoices that make the nature of each service obvious, and review the mix annually before filing Form 151.

Exception 3: become a company director

The most used route in practice: incorporate an SL (Spanish limited company), appoint yourself director, and enter the regime through that status. Since 2023 there is no shareholding cap (you can own 100%), with one exception: if the company is an asset-holding entity (over half its assets not used in a business activity), your stake must stay below 25%.

Done properly, this lets a consultant operate through a company with a director’s salary taxed at 24%. But it demands real substance: genuine business activity, market-consistent remuneration, and clean corporate and tax compliance. An empty SL set up purely for the regime is an invitation to an audit, and a company whose only asset is cash or a portfolio is an asset-holding entity, with the 25% cap.

What you need to show: the incorporation and your appointment as director dated consistently with the move, an operating business, and a salary that a third party would recognise as pay for the job.

Watch-outs: the company’s own obligations (corporate tax, VAT, accounting, payroll) are real costs; and if you also own a foreign company that pays you, the two structures must be read together. Owner-employees of a US LLC or S-corp are a special case, see Beckham Law with a US LLC or S-corp.

The order of steps that keeps the regime alive

The same facts in a different order produce a different outcome. This is the sequence that works for each route:

  1. Decide the route before you land. The route determines what you register, when, and which date starts the 6-month clock.
  2. Create the access circumstance first. Sign the contract, obtain the permit, incorporate the SL and appoint yourself director, or secure the ENISA report or startup client, before any registration as an ordinary autónomo.
  3. Register with Social Security under that circumstance. For a director, that is the corresponding regime for directors; for an employee, the general regime through the employer. Note the date: it starts the 6 months.
  4. File Form 149 within the first weeks. See the deadline guide.
  5. Only then add anything else, and check first that it does not create a permanent establishment or push you over a threshold that ends the regime.

The two routes that do not work

The nomad visa on the self-employed track. The digital nomad permit has an employee track and a freelancer track. The tax regime’s remote-work route covers employees of a foreign company; the permit’s freelancer track lets you live and work from Spain but does not by itself open the regime. Founders often discover this after the visa is granted. See the nomad visa with your own company.

Invoicing through a foreign entity “from abroad”. If you do the work from Spain, for clients, through a single-member foreign company, AEAT can read that as business income obtained through a permanent establishment in Spain, the exact exclusion. Where the company is real, has substance and pays you a genuine salary as its employee, the remote-work route may fit; where it is a pass-through for your own freelancing, it does not.

What to do if you already registered as autónomo

The order of events now works against you, but the answer depends on the facts rather than on the label:

  • If you have not yet filed Form 149 and are inside the 6 months, the question is whether a valid route can still be evidenced, an SL and directorship, a startup client meeting the 40% test, an ENISA report, and whether the existing activity can be closed or absorbed without leaving a permanent establishment behind. This is a case for a professional review before filing anything else, because the file will be read as a whole.
  • If you are already inside the regime on an employment route and are thinking of adding self-employed side work, the risk is a later loss of the regime, which must be notified within one month and cannot be undone. Ask before you register, not after.
  • If the deadline has already passed, the regime is not available for this relocation; the useful work is planning the general income tax properly.

Is it worth the effort?

Only your numbers can answer that. A director’s salary of €90,000 taxed at 24% versus the progressive scale saves roughly €6,300 a year in our savings calculator (illustrative: single taxpayer, employment income only, Catalan scale); at €150,000 the difference is around €19,500 a year, before the cost of running the company. At moderate incomes, with family allowances you would be giving up, the general regime can win. Run your figures in the savings calculator before you incorporate anything.

Your route determines the order of the steps (registration, incorporation, application) and the deadlines. It’s the decision to make before you land, not after. Frequently asked questions

Can I invoice as a freelancer while under the Beckham Law? +

Under the general route (employment contract), a later autónomo registration for side work can endanger the regime if it is read as a permanent establishment. Under the entrepreneur or qualified-professional routes, the self-employed activity itself is the entry door. Each combination needs analysis.

What is the ENISA favourable report for the Beckham Law? +

It is the certification that your activity is entrepreneurial and innovative with special economic interest for Spain. It is requested online and is a prerequisite for the entrepreneur route into the impatriate regime.

Can the director of their own SL company use the Beckham Law? +

Yes, being a company director has been a valid access route since 2023 with no shareholding limit, unless the company is an asset-holding entity, in which case your stake must be below 25%.

I already registered as autónomo when I arrived. Is the regime lost? +

Not automatically, but the order of events now works against you and the file needs careful analysis. What matters is whether your move can still be shown to be caused by a valid route and whether you are earning income through a permanent establishment. This is a case for a professional review before filing anything else.

Does the digital nomad visa on the freelancer track give me the Beckham Law? +

No. The remote-work route into the regime covers employees of a foreign company. A nomad visa granted on the self-employed track lets you live and work from Spain but does not by itself open the tax regime; you would need one of the three routes described here.
